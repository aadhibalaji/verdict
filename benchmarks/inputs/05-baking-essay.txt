My mother says I ruined my first birthday cake before I could walk, by reaching into the bowl of frosting on the counter while she wasn't looking. I don't remember that, but I believe it, because I have been reaching into bowls I'm not supposed to touch ever since.

I started baking seriously in tenth grade after a bad batch of macarons, the kind with the cracked tops and hollow shells that every baking blog warns you about. I made the same recipe eleven times over three months, adjusting oven temperature by five-degree increments and writing the results in a notebook my chemistry teacher would have been proud of, if she'd known it was for cookies and not for her class.

By the twelfth batch, they were perfect: smooth domes, ruffled feet, a shell that gave way to something chewy. I brought them to school and nobody noticed anything special about them, which was somehow the best part. They were just cookies to everyone else. To me they were eleven failures standing behind one success, invisible to anyone who didn't know to look.

I started selling them at the farmer's market that spring, badly at first — I priced them too low, I ran out of the vanilla ones by 9am every week, I once forgot the salt in an entire batch of caramel ones and sold them anyway because I couldn't afford to throw out sixty dollars of ingredients. I've since learned to price for my time, not just my ingredients, and to make a few less of the flavor that always disappears first so it lasts the whole morning.

I don't know if I want to study business or chemistry in college, and macarons haven't resolved that for me. What they've given me is proof that I can be bad at something in private for months without giving up on it, which feels more useful than knowing what I want to major in.
