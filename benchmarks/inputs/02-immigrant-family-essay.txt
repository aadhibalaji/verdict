My grandmother crossed the border with three dollars and a suitcase of dried chilies. I have heard this sentence so many times at family dinners that I used to roll my eyes when she started the story again.

This year, for a history project, I actually recorded her telling it, start to finish, on my phone. It took ninety minutes and she cried twice. She told me things I had never heard before: that she left behind a sister she never saw again, that she worked in a chicken processing plant for six years before she opened her own restaurant, that the recipe for the chile she still cooks every Sunday is not actually her mother's recipe but one she invented because she couldn't remember the original correctly and was too proud to admit it.

I used to think my job in this family was to be the first one to go to college, as if that fact alone would be enough to make her sacrifice mean something. After the recording, I think my job is smaller and harder than that: to actually listen to her instead of finishing her sentences in my head while she talks.

I am applying to study public health because I want to understand why women like my grandmother, who worked six days a week for thirty years, so often can't get a doctor to take their pain seriously until it's an emergency. I don't have a tidy conclusion. I have a recording, ninety minutes long, that I haven't fully listened back to yet because I'm scared of what else is in it.
